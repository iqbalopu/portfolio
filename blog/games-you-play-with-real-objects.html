Games you play with real objects

Alienide Interactive, 2017–2019 · XR & physical interfaces · ~7 min read

Early in my career I spent two years building games where the controller wasn't a controller. It was a surfboard someone had built, or a bicycle, or a cricket bat with a Vive tracker bolted into the handle. It remains the strangest and most instructive work I've done.

These were location-based experiences — installations, not products you download. Someone walks up, gets on a physical thing, and plays. That framing changes almost everything about how the software has to behave.

The catalogue

  • Big Kahuna (released as Into the Blue) — a VR surf simulator. You stand on an actual surfboard and a tracker turns your balance into steering.
  • Mojo Pitha Chor — a chase game you play by pedalling a real bicycle through a recreation of Old Dhaka, complete with an automated traffic system.
  • Wing Glider — you lie down on a purpose-built glider rig to fly.
  • Clemon Cricket — partial VR with no headset at all: a real cricket bat with a controller in the handle. 25 runs in 2 overs.
  • Fruit Ninja VR — the mobile classic, rebuilt for the Vive.

There is no documentation for a surfboard

When you build a normal game, almost every problem you hit has been hit before. There's a forum thread, a Unity issue, a Stack Overflow answer from 2016 with a green tick on it.

When the input device is a plywood surfboard with a tracker screwed to it, none of that exists. Not because the problem is intellectually harder, but because you are the first person to have this specific object in this specific room. Every question — how much does the board tilt before a rider means to turn, what counts as a pedal stroke versus a wobble, where exactly is the bat's sweet spot in tracker space — has to be answered empirically, by you, with the thing in front of you.

It's the closest game development gets to engineering against reality.

You end up building calibration tooling before you build gameplay. Not because it's good practice, but because nothing works until the software agrees with the physical object about where it is and what it's doing.

Physical rigs drift, and people are not careful

A gamepad is the same on day one and day two hundred. A physical rig isn't. Mountings loosen. Someone moves the base station to plug in a vacuum cleaner. The board gets stood on by somebody twice the weight of anyone you tested with. A tracker's battery dies mid-session, and the recovery behaviour of your game is now a customer-facing feature.

So you learn to write software that assumes its own inputs are unreliable and its environment is being rearranged behind your back. Recalibration can't be a developer tool; it has to be something the person running the installation can do in thirty seconds between players, without instruction.

The thing that changed how I work

Installations mean you stand next to strangers while they play.

I cannot overstate what this does to you as a developer. You watch someone approach the bicycle and hesitate because it isn't obvious they're allowed to touch it. You watch someone lean on the surfboard in a direction you never considered. You watch a person completely miss the instruction you spent a week wording, because they were looking at the screen, not the sign. You see the exact half-second where somebody understands the game, or doesn't.

Most developers never get this. You ship into an analytics dashboard and you infer: retention dipped at this step, so something is wrong there, probably. Inference is a genuine skill and I use it every day now on live mobile titles. But watching someone's hands is worth an enormous amount of inference, and having done both, I think the hands teach you faster.

And the deadline does not move

A lot of this work was branded — arcade titles for Skitto (Grameenphone), a road-safety game for Unilever, an AR app for a furniture retailer. Branded work has a property that product work doesn't: a campaign has a date, and the date is not negotiable. The event happens whether you are ready or not.

That's uncomfortable and it's good for you. It forces you to be honest much earlier about what will actually be finished, and it kills the instinct to keep polishing something that's already fine at the expense of something that isn't built yet.

What I carried into mobile

I work on competitive mobile games now — very different constraints, no plywood involved. But three things came with me:

  • Assume your inputs are lying. On a rig it was a drifting tracker. On a real-money mobile title it's the client, which should never be the source of truth about the rules. Same instinct, different threat.
  • Recovery is a feature. What the software does when something has gone wrong is part of the product, not an edge case to handle later.
  • Watch people, not just dashboards. Whenever I can get in front of an actual player, I take it.

I'd build another one of these tomorrow.
