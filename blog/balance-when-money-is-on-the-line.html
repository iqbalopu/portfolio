When there's money on the result, balance stops being a feeling

Brick Blaster Cash · competitive mobile · ~6 min read

Every game developer tunes by feel. You play it, something is off, you nudge a number, you play it again. That loop works for most of the games I've built. It stopped working the first time a match had real money on the outcome.

Brick Blaster Cash is a brick breaker. You've played one; the rules take about four seconds to explain. The difference is that two players enter the same match, pay to be there, and one of them walks away with a prize. That single change reaches back through every design decision in the game, and the first place it lands is balance.

The thing a cash match has to guarantee

In a normal free-to-play game, "is this fair?" is a question about player sentiment. If the difficulty curve is a little generous on level 12, nobody is harmed. The worst case is that someone gets bored.

In a skill-based cash match, fairness is the product. What you are actually promising is this: the better player wins more often than the worse player, and the gap between two players' scores reflects the gap in their skill rather than the gap in their luck. If that promise breaks, you no longer have a skill game. You have something else, and "something else" is both a design failure and a regulatory one.

So the question changes shape. It is no longer "does this feel good to play?" — though it still has to. It becomes "what does the distribution of scores look like across thousands of matches, and what does a change to this number do to that distribution?"

Every tuning change moves the whole curve

This is the part that surprised me most. In a single-player game a tuning change is local: you make the paddle faster, the game gets easier, you move on. In a competitive game, the paddle speed is an input to a score distribution, and the score distribution is what decides whether a match feels fair.

Make a mechanic slightly more generous and you don't simply shift every score upward. You change the shape. A more forgiving mechanic compresses the distribution — good players and mediocre players end up closer together, which means more matches are decided by the last few seconds, which means more matches are effectively decided by luck. That is the opposite of what a skill game needs, and you'd never notice it by playing the build yourself a dozen times.

Tuning stops being a feel decision and becomes a statistical one.

Conversely, a harsher mechanic spreads the distribution out. Now skill dominates, which sounds ideal, until you notice that a new player has no path to ever winning, quits in week one, and takes the size of your matchmaking pool with them. A competitive game needs new players to lose in a way that feels close.

The balance point between those two failures is not a matter of taste. It is a number you find by looking at what real players actually score.

What this changes about how you work

Three practical consequences, all of which took me a while to internalise:

You cannot tune from your own play sessions

You are the worst possible test subject. You know the mechanic intimately, you have played the build a thousand times, and your scores sit in a tail of the distribution that almost no real player occupies. Your feel is real information about whether the game is pleasant, and almost no information about whether it is fair.

A tuning change is a release-shaped risk

If a number that decides payouts is baked into the client, then changing it means a store submission, a review queue, and a population split across two versions with two different difficulty curves — which, in a game where people compete against each other, is unacceptable. This is the reason those values live on the server and not in the build, and it is a large enough topic that I've written about it separately.

"It feels better" is the beginning of the argument, not the end

The instinct is still worth a great deal — it is usually what tells you where to look. But on a competitive title the instinct produces a hypothesis, and the hypothesis has to survive contact with the distribution before it ships.

The part nobody warns you about

There's a second balance problem sitting behind the first one, and it isn't about the game at all. A prize structure has to be sustainable. The distribution of scores determines how often players at each skill level win, which determines what the prize pool has to pay out, which determines whether the whole thing works as a business. Change the mechanic and you have moved a number on a spreadsheet in the finance conversation.

I don't think I appreciated, before working on these games, how tightly the feel of a mechanic is coupled to the economics of the product. In a premium game they're separated by a purchase. In a free-to-play game they're separated by a monetisation layer. In a skill-based cash game they are the same decision.

What I'd tell myself in 2023

Keep tuning by feel — it's still how you find the interesting candidates. But treat the feel as the start of an investigation rather than the conclusion of one, get the values off the client early so that investigating is cheap, and look at what players actually score rather than what you score.

The mechanic is the fun part. The distribution is the product.
